The Clock

What a Warm Walk-In Actually Costs You

A home refrigerator going down is an inconvenience. A walk-in going down is a balance-sheet event. A modest 8×10 box in a working restaurant carries of product on an ordinary Thursday; a grocery or catering operation, several times that.

Texas Food Establishment Rules follow the FDA Food Code, and the number that matters is 41°F. Time/temperature control for safety foods — dairy, cut produce, raw protein, cooked items held cold — must sit at or below it. Past 41°F the Food Code allows a four-hour window under time-as-a-public-health-control before the product is discarded — and that window only counts if you were tracking it. An inspector from Dallas County or Tarrant County Public Health who finds product at 48°F and no temperature log does not care why the box failed.

So the cost stacks three ways: product, closed hours, inspection risk. Which is why our first question is not “what brand is it” — it is what is the box reading, and what is in it.

Stabilize the box before we arrive

Stop opening the door — every entry dumps the load and drags the coil further behind. Look at the condensing unit: if the coil is buried in grease and dust, blowing it out from the clean side can drop head pressure enough to start pulling temperature again while you wait. If the evaporator is a solid block of ice, do not chip at it — a screwdriver through an evaporator tube turns a small service call into a refrigerant-recovery job.

Failure Modes

What Actually Breaks on a Walk-In

Walk-ins fail in a short list of predictable ways. Which one you are looking at decides whether this is a same-visit fix or a parts-order job.

Iced-over evaporator, stuck defrost cycle

The most common walk-in call. A failed defrost timer, heater or termination thermostat lets frost build until the coil cannot move air. Which is why “running but not cooling” usually means defrost, not compressor.

Fouled condenser

Grease, flour and dust blanket the coil, head pressure climbs, capacity drops long before the unit quits. The failure preventive maintenance actually prevents.

Refrigerant leak

Vibration-cracked line sets, failed schrader cores, evaporator tubing corroded by condensate. Low charge shows as long run times and a coil icing in one spot. Topping off without finding the leak is money burned.

Compressor failure

Blamed far more often than it happens. Before condemning one we check start components, contactor and incoming voltage — a bad capacitor imitates a dead compressor at a fraction of the cost.

Welded or chattering contactor

A wear part switching every cycle. Welded contacts leave the compressor running with nothing to stop it; pitted contacts short-cycle and eventually take it with them.

Failed thermostat or temperature probe

A drifted probe reports 36°F while the box sits at 47°F. This is what gets operators written up — the display looks fine. Trust the product over the readout.

Blocked condensate drain line

Drain clogs or heat tape fails, water backs into the box, and you get a sheet of ice and a slip hazard at the door. Common in freezers, where the line freezes at the wall.

Doors, gaskets, hinges and closers

The cheapest fix here and the most ignored. A torn gasket or a door that will not latch runs the system continuously and ices the coil. If the door needs a shoulder, the refrigeration is paying for it.

The Money Question

Repair or Replace a Commercial Box

The arithmetic here is not the residential arithmetic, and the home rule of thumb costs you money in both directions.

The box and the machine are separate decisions. Panels, floor and door are structure — routinely twenty-five years, worth repairing well past the point the refrigeration is worn out. The condensing unit and evaporator on top are mechanical equipment on a ten-to-fifteen-year curve. A twenty-year-old box with a dying condensing unit is usually a re-power, not a replacement.

Refrigerant decides more than age does. R-22 has not been produced or imported in the US since 2020 — only reclaimed stock, priced accordingly. A significant leak repair plus a full R-22 charge on an aging unit is often the moment to move to R-448A/R-449A, or a self-contained R-290 unit on a small box. Already on a modern refrigerant? Repair almost always wins.

You get the comparison in writing before anything is ordered: repair cost, re-power cost, remaining life. If replacing is the better spend we say so — same as on compressor jobs, where the honest answer is often not the bigger invoice.

Yes. We hold standing service and maintenance agreements with multi-site operators across DFW — coworking, restaurant and hospitality groups — covering dozens of locations under one point of contact.

Can you give me documentation for the health department or an insurance claim?

Yes. Every visit produces a written service record: equipment, fault found, work performed, temperatures on arrival and at completion. That is the document an inspector or adjuster asks for.

My product may already be spoiled. Is it still worth calling?

Yes, and quickly. The loss is already fixed, but the box has to be back at temperature and documented before you restock — otherwise you repeat it on the next delivery. Discard decisions are yours and your health authority's; we supply the arrival temperature in writing.

Do you service walk-in freezers too, or only coolers?

Both, plus commercial ice machines, reach-ins, prep tables and the rest of the back-of-house line. Freezers add defrost and drain-heat faults coolers do not have — which is why the defrost circuit is checked first there.

The on-site diagnostic is credited back in full when you approve the repair, and you get a flat-rate quote before a part is ordered. We do not publish a range and do not quote by phone — a welded contactor and a sealed-system leak repair on an R-22 unit are an order of magnitude apart.
