Short Answer

Why a Refrigerator Stops Cooling

Most refrigerators stop cooling for one of five reasons: a condenser coil choked with dust, a failed evaporator fan, a defrost system that has let ice bridge the evaporator, a door seal that no longer closes, or a drifted temperature sensor. A failed compressor is far down that list — it gets blamed constantly and is rarely what we find.

That matters because four of those five are inexpensive, and the fifth is the one every vendor reaches for first. If someone quotes you a compressor in the first ten minutes without gauges on the system, get a second opinion.

The single most useful thing you can tell us on the phone is which compartment is warm. A freezer that is still cold with a warm refrigerator is an airflow problem almost every time — not refrigerant, not the compressor. Both compartments warm together is a different diagnosis. That one detail often decides what goes on the truck.

Do This First

Four Checks Before You Pay for a Service Call

Ten minutes, no tools, and they genuinely resolve a share of the calls we get.

  1. Clean the condenser coil. It is behind a grille at the bottom front, or behind the unit, or at the top on a built-in column. In DFW homes with pets it clogs fast, and a blanketed coil cannot shed heat — so the box runs warm and the compressor works itself to death. Vacuum it gently and give it 24 hours before judging.
  2. Check the door seal. Close the door on a dollar bill and pull. If it slides out with no resistance, the gasket is leaking warm humid air in continuously — which is both a “runs constantly” complaint and a “why is there ice everywhere” complaint.
  3. Confirm airflow inside. Boxes stacked against the rear vents stop cold air reaching the refrigerator compartment. It produces exactly the same symptom as a failed fan and costs nothing to rule out.
  4. Put a thermometer in a glass of water on the middle shelf. Leave it a few hours and compare with the display. A drifted sensor is a common and inexpensive fault, but you cannot see it without an independent reading — the panel will insist everything is fine.

Still warm after 24 hours? Call (469) 224-0577 and lead with which compartment is warm and what the thermometer says.

One thing not to do: do not keep running a unit that is clearly failing while you decide. On a sealed-system fault, continuing to run it can turn a leak repair into a compressor replacement.

Standard freestanding

Samsung, LG, Whirlpool, GE, Frigidaire, Maytag, KitchenAid. Single sealed system, wheels out in minutes. Here the maths is different: on an older unit with a major sealed-system fault, replacement is sometimes the honest answer, and we will say so.

The practical difference for you is the repair-versus-replace line. On a built-in it sits a long way out — these are units where a fan, a sensor or a defrost component is a sensible fix on a fifteen-year-old machine. On a standard unit the line is much closer in, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling.

The Decision

Repair or Replace a Refrigerator

On a built-in or integrated unit, repair almost always wins — the cabinet is fitted to the kitchen and replacement drags cabinetry work in with it. On a standard freestanding unit the line is much closer: a fan, sensor, defrost component or start relay is worth fixing at almost any age, but a major sealed-system job on an older mass-market fridge often is not.

What actually decides it:

  • Which part failed, not the age on the label. Fans, sensors, defrost heaters, gaskets and start components are routine fixes on old machines. Sealed-system work is the expensive category, and it is the one worth thinking about.
  • Whether the part still exists. On discontinued control boards, availability decides it, not price. We tell you that before you spend the diagnostic, not after the part is on order.
  • What replacing actually costs you. On a built-in that includes the cabinetry and the panels, not just the appliance. People routinely forget the second half.
  • Whether it has been failing for a while. A unit that has been nursed along for a year with a dying compressor is a different proposition from one that failed clean last week.

You get the comparison before anything is ordered. If replacing is the better spend we say so — the same way we do on compressor jobs, where the honest answer is often not the bigger invoice.

FAQ

Refrigerator Repair Questions

My freezer is cold but the refrigerator is warm. What causes that?

Airflow, almost every time — not refrigerant and not the compressor. Cold air is made in the freezer and moved across to the refrigerator by the evaporator fan. If that fan fails, or the defrost system lets ice bridge the evaporator coil, the freezer stays cold while the refrigerator climbs. The compressor keeps running, which is why it sounds like the unit is working perfectly. It is one of the most common calls we run and usually not an expensive one.

How much does refrigerator repair cost?

We don't publish repair prices and we don't quote them by phone. You get an on-site diagnostic first, then a flat-rate quote in writing before any work starts or any part is ordered, and the diagnostic fee is waived when you go ahead with the repair. The reason is that the same complaint — “it's not cooling” — can be a door gasket or a sealed-system job, and a number given before anyone has seen the unit is a guess.

Is my refrigerator worth repairing?

On a built-in or integrated unit, almost always yes — it is fitted to a cabinet cutout, so replacing it pulls cabinetry work in with it, and most failures are repairable fans, sensors, defrost components and gaskets. On a standard freestanding unit the answer depends on what failed: those same parts are worth fixing at almost any age, but a major sealed-system job on an older mass-market fridge often is not. We give you the comparison before anything is ordered.

There's ice building up on the back wall of my freezer. Why?

The defrost system has stopped clearing the evaporator. A failed defrost heater, timer or termination thermostat lets frost build until it bridges the coil and blocks airflow — which is also why the refrigerator side goes warm. A door that is not sealing adds moisture and accelerates it. Do not chip at the ice: a screwdriver through an evaporator tube turns a small repair into a sealed-system job.

Do I really need a technician, or can I clean the coils myself?

Clean them yourself — genuinely. A condenser choked with dust is the most common cause of a refrigerator that slowly stops keeping up, and it needs no tools. Pull the grille, vacuum the coil, give it 24 hours. If it is still warm after that, the cheap causes are ruled out and the visit will be shorter because of it.

Do you work on built-in and integrated refrigerators?

Yes, and it's most of what we do. Sub-Zero, Wolf, Viking, Thermador, GE Monogram, JennAir, Dacor and Miele built-ins and columns. That includes getting the unit out and back without marking the surround, which on cabinet-fitted equipment is half the job and the part a general appliance tech is often not set up for.

Do you do sealed-system work, or subcontract it?

We do it ourselves. Refrigerant leak location, recovery, recharge and compressor replacement are performed by our own EPA-certified technician rather than handed to a subcontractor. That is the practical difference between a repair this week and a repair in two weeks, and it is worth asking any appliance company before you book.

My refrigerator is running constantly. Is that bad?

It means the system is not satisfying, and it is worth looking at now rather than later. The usual causes are a door seal letting warm humid air in, a condenser coil that cannot shed heat, or a control that is not cycling. All three are fixable. Left alone, constant running is also how a marginal sealed system becomes a failed compressor.
