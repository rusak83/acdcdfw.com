Short Answer

What Actually Fails on a DFW Air Conditioner

In rough order of how often we find them: a failed run capacitor, a burnt contactor, a condenser coil choked with cottonwood and dust, an airflow restriction at the filter or the return, and only then a refrigerant leak. Four of those five are electrical or mechanical faults on a system that still holds a full charge — which is why “you’re low on freon” is the diagnosis we most often have to undo.

A diagnostic visit exists to answer three questions in order. Is the system getting power and a call for cooling? Is it moving air? Is it moving heat? Those are three different failures with three different repairs, and they present almost identically from the hallway — the house is warm and the thermostat says 72.

The measurement below is the sort of thing that settles it quickly. Supply air leaving a register at 59.5°F against a 77.1°F ceiling means the refrigeration circuit is doing its job; if the house is still hot, the problem is distribution, not the equipment outside.

Do This First

Four Checks Before You Pay for a Service Call

  1. Change the filter and look at the one you pulled out. If light does not pass through it, you have found at least part of the problem. A loaded filter starves the coil of air, which drops capacity, freezes the coil, and eventually looks exactly like a refrigerant fault. This one check resolves a meaningful share of “not cooling” calls at no cost.
  2. Check the thermostat properly. Fresh batteries, set to COOL, fan on AUTO, and setpoint at least three degrees below the room. Then confirm the little screen is actually calling — a thermostat that has lost its schedule will hold a setback temperature all afternoon and look like a dead system.
  3. Check the breaker and the outdoor disconnect. There are two places power can be interrupted: the panel inside and the pull-out disconnect box mounted next to the condenser. Reset a tripped breaker once. If it trips again, stop and call — something downstream is drawing current it should not.
  4. Walk the house and open the returns. Closed vents in unused rooms and furniture pushed against a return both raise static pressure across the system. That shows up as poor airflow, uneven rooms, and a blower working harder than it was designed to.

Still not right after those four? Call (469) 224-0577 and lead with what you found — filter condition, whether the outdoor fan is spinning, and whether there is ice anywhere. That is most of a diagnosis already.

We Diagnose and Maintain. We Don’t Sell You a New System.

This is a diagnostics and maintenance service, not a replacement business. We test, measure, and repair components: capacitors, contactors, fan and blower motors, condensate systems, thermostats and controls, coil cleaning, and the electrical faults that account for most summer breakdowns. We also tell you plainly when a system is genuinely at the end of its life.

What we do not do is quote you a new system. That matters because of how this trade usually works: the company diagnosing the fault is very often the company selling the replacement, and that is a conflict of interest sitting in your hallway. A capacitor is a small repair. A new condensing unit is not. When the same person decides which one you need and profits from the answer, you are entitled to a second opinion — and quite often we are that second opinion.

If the honest answer is that your system should be replaced, you get that answer, the reasoning behind it, and the measurements it rests on. Then you take it to whoever you like for the install. Nobody here earns anything from that outcome, which is exactly why the answer is worth something.

The One to Push Back On

“You’re Low on Refrigerant” Is Not a Diagnosis

A sealed refrigeration circuit does not consume refrigerant. If a system is low, it leaked — and a top-up without finding the leak is buying the identical repair again next summer, plus the cost of the gas twice.

This is worth knowing because it is the most commonly oversold item in residential HVAC. The symptom that gets it blamed — poor cooling on a hot day — is produced just as readily by a filthy condenser, a weak capacitor letting the compressor struggle, or an airflow restriction indoors. Those are cheaper, more common, and testable in minutes.

So when a charge is genuinely low, the useful work is finding out where it went: the service valves, a coil, a braze joint, or a line set run through a wall. That is a longer conversation than adding refrigerant, and a considerably better one. If somebody has topped your system up two summers running without ever mentioning a leak search, you have been sold gas rather than a repair.

Local Conditions

What North Texas Does to HVAC Equipment

Equipment here does not fail the way the manuals imagine. Three local factors do most of the damage.

Heat load and run hours. A DFW condenser runs long cycles for five months a year in ambient temperatures its capacitors were rated at the edge of. Capacitors are heat-sensitive by nature, which is why they are the most replaced part in this market by a wide margin, and why a unit that was fine in May quits in the second week of July.

Attic air handlers. A great many DFW homes put the air handler and its condensate pan in the attic, directly above finished ceilings. That is convenient for the builder and unforgiving later: a drain line that clogs with algae does not spill onto a garage floor, it spills into drywall. The float switch that should prevent this is a five-dollar part that fails silently, so on a maintenance visit it gets tested rather than admired.

Spring debris. Cottonwood, oak pollen and construction dust mat into the outdoor coil through April and May. Nobody notices, because the system is barely working. The bill arrives in July as a unit that cannot reject heat, runs constantly, and gets diagnosed by somebody else as low on refrigerant.

FAQ

HVAC Diagnostic Questions

What does an HVAC diagnostic visit actually include?

Electrical testing at the disconnect and the contactor, capacitor measurement under load, temperature split across the coil, static pressure and airflow assessment, condensate and drain check, and a look at the condition of both coils. You get the readings, not just a verdict. It is a $90 on-site diagnostic, and that fee is waived when you go ahead with the repair.

My outdoor unit is humming but the fan isn’t turning. What is that?

Most often a failed run capacitor, sometimes a seized fan motor or a contactor not making proper contact. Switch the system off at the thermostat until someone looks at it. A compressor that keeps running while the condenser fan is stopped has no way to reject heat, and that turns an inexpensive part into an expensive one fairly quickly.

There is ice on the pipe going into the house. Should I keep it running?

No. Switch the system to FAN ONLY and let the ice melt completely, which usually takes a few hours. Running a frozen system risks liquid refrigerant reaching the compressor. Icing is almost always an airflow restriction first and a low charge second, and nobody can tell which while it is buried under ice.

Do you replace air conditioning systems?

No, and that is deliberate. We are a diagnostics, repair and maintenance service. We will tell you honestly when a system is finished and explain why, but the replacement quote comes from somebody else. It means our diagnosis has nothing riding on the answer being expensive.

Another company said I need refrigerant every year. Is that normal?

It is common and it is not normal. A sealed system does not use refrigerant up. If it needs topping up annually, it has a leak that nobody has located, and you are paying for the same repair repeatedly. Finding the leak is the actual work; adding gas is postponing it.

How much does HVAC repair cost?

We don’t publish repair prices and we don’t quote them by phone. The $90 diagnostic comes first, then a flat-rate quote in writing before any work starts, and the diagnostic fee is waived if you proceed with the repair. The same complaint can be a capacitor or a compressor, and any number offered before someone has measured the system is a guess.

How often should an air conditioner be serviced in Texas?

Twice a year suits this climate better than once: before cooling season and before heating season. Our run hours are long and our spring debris load is heavy, so the two things that pay for themselves are a clean condenser coil and a clear condensate drain. Filters need attention considerably more often than that, particularly in homes with pets.

There’s a water stain on the ceiling under my attic unit. How urgent is it?

Urgent. That is a blocked condensate drain, a failed float switch, or a corroded secondary pan, and the water has already found its way through the ceiling. Turn the system off and call. The drywall repair is usually the cheapest part of what happens if it keeps running.

Do you work on heat pumps and furnaces, or only air conditioning?

Both. Gas furnaces, electric strip heat and heat pumps, including reversing valve and defrost control faults. North Texas heating seasons are short, which means heating faults typically sit undetected from March until the first genuinely cold night in November.
