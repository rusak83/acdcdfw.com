Failure Modes

What Actually Fails on Commercial Refrigeration

Self-contained commercial equipment fails in a short and predictable list. Which fault you are looking at decides whether this is fixed on the first visit or waits on a part — and it is worth knowing before someone tells you the compressor is dead.

Fouled condenser

The single most common cause of “it runs but it is not cold.” Kitchen grease and flour blanket the coil, head pressure climbs, and capacity falls off long before the unit actually quits. This is the failure a maintenance contract exists to prevent.

Iced evaporator and defrost faults

A failed defrost timer, heater or termination thermostat lets frost build until the coil stops moving air. Running but warm usually points here, not at the compressor. On prep tables the ice hides under the rail where nobody looks.

Start components mistaken for a dead compressor

A failed capacitor, start relay or overload imitates a dead compressor exactly: a hum, a click, and no cooling. We check start components, contactor and incoming voltage before condemning anything — the difference is an order of magnitude on the invoice.

Refrigerant leaks

Vibration-cracked lines, failed schrader cores, corroded evaporator tubing. Low charge shows as long run times and a coil icing in one spot only. Topping a system off without locating the leak is money burned twice.

Condenser and evaporator fan failures

A seized or slowing fan motor takes the whole system down with it — no airflow across the condenser means no heat rejection, no airflow across the evaporator means the box stratifies and ices. Cheap part, expensive if it is left running.

Drifted thermostats and temperature probes

The fault that gets operators written up, because the display looks fine. A drifted probe reports 36°F while the pan wells sit at 47°F. If your product feels wrong, trust the product and a calibrated thermometer over the readout.

Blocked condensate drains and pans

A clogged drain line or failed heat tape backs water into the cabinet, ices the floor of a freezer, and puts a slip hazard in front of the door. Common where the line runs cold through an exterior wall.

Doors, gaskets, hinges and closers

The cheapest repair on this list and the most ignored. A torn gasket or a door that will not latch runs the system continuously and ices the coil. If a door needs a shoulder to close, the refrigeration is paying for it every hour you are open.

The Decision

Repair or Replace Commercial Equipment

The residential rule of thumb — half the price of new, throw it out — costs you money in both directions on commercial equipment. Three things actually decide it.

What failed, not how old it is. A ten-year-old True reach-in with a failed fan motor, contactor or start component is a cheap repair on a cabinet built to outlast three of them. The same cabinet with a burned-out compressor and a corroded evaporator is a different conversation. Age alone condemns nothing.

Refrigerant decides more than age does. R-22 has not been produced or imported in the US since 2020 — only reclaimed stock, priced accordingly. A significant leak repair plus a full R-22 charge on an aging unit is often the moment to move to R-448A/R-449A, or a self-contained R-290 unit on smaller equipment. Already on a modern refrigerant? Repair almost always wins.

Parts availability, not the parts price. On discontinued control boards and proprietary evaporators the question stops being what it costs and becomes whether it exists. We tell you that before you spend the diagnostic, not after the part is on order.

You get the comparison in writing before anything is ordered: repair cost, remaining life, and what replacing would actually run. If replacing is the better spend we say so — the same way we do on compressor jobs, where the honest answer is often not the bigger invoice.
