7 Warning Signs Your Commercial Refrigeration Is About to Fail (A DFW Owner’s Checklist)

October 6, 2026 · AC/DC Refrigeration & Appliance Repair

Commercial refrigeration almost never dies without warning. It tells you for days, sometimes weeks, and the signals are visible to anyone who works the line — they just do not look like emergencies yet. Below are the seven we see most often on service calls across Dallas–Fort Worth, in roughly the order they show up, plus what you can check yourself in five minutes and the point where waiting stops being free.

1. The compressor starts and stops every few minutes

A healthy box pulls down, satisfies, and rests. A box in trouble runs in short bursts — on for two or three minutes, off, on again. Short cycling is not a quirk; it is the system failing to reach its setpoint and giving up, or a control telling it to stop early. Every one of those starts draws several times the running current, which is why short cycling and a hot compressor usually arrive together. It is the single most reliable early warning we see, and the one owners most often tune out because the box is still cold.

2. Ice on the evaporator inside the box

A light frost that disappears after a defrost cycle is normal. A coil with a solid white shell on it is not. Ice insulates the coil from the air it is supposed to be cooling, so the system runs longer and longer to do less and less. By the time the ice is thick enough to see from the doorway, airflow through the coil is already down significantly.

3. Water on the floor under the unit

Condensate has one route out: the drain pan, the drain line, and in most walk-ins a heated drain so it does not freeze on the way. Water on the floor means that route is blocked, frozen, or the pan is overflowing because the coil is carrying more ice than the pan can take when it melts. In a kitchen it is also a slip hazard and a Health Department note waiting to happen, so it tends to get mopped rather than reported — which is how it goes unfixed for months.

4. The fans got louder, or one of them stopped

Evaporator and condenser fan motors usually announce themselves before they quit: a rattle, a hum that changes pitch, a blade that ticks against a shroud. A condenser fan that has quietly died is especially expensive, because the compressor keeps running against head pressure it cannot shed. Count the fans you can see and listen once a week; it costs nothing and catches a cheap part before it takes an expensive one with it.

5. Temperature drifts in the evening and recovers overnight

This is the sign that gets dismissed most often, because the box reads fine at open. A system that is marginal — dirty condenser, slightly low charge, a fan that is not moving what it used to — can still hold temperature in a cool, quiet kitchen at 6 AM and lose ground during dinner service when the door opens constantly and the ambient in the kitchen climbs. If your controller reads two or three degrees higher at 8 PM than it does at 8 AM, the system has no reserve left.

6. Oily film at a fitting, a valve, or along the suction line

Refrigerant carries compressor oil with it. When it escapes, the gas goes and the oil stays, so a slow leak leaves a damp, dust-collecting smudge at the joint it left from. A sealed circuit does not consume refrigerant — if a system is low, it leaked. Topping it up without finding where buys you the identical repair again in a few months, which is why we chase the leak rather than the gauge reading.

7. The power bill climbed and nothing else changed

A fouled condenser, an iced evaporator and a tired compressor all do the same thing to your electricity cost: they make the equipment run longer for the same result. If your consumption is up and your hours, menu and equipment list are not, refrigeration is the first place to look. It is the slowest signal of the seven and usually confirms what the other six already said.

What you can check yourself in five minutes

  • Door gaskets. Close the door on a dollar bill and pull. If it slides out with no drag, that section of gasket is not sealing. Walk the whole perimeter; one bad corner is enough to ice a coil.
  • The condenser. Find the finned coil — on a reach-in it is usually behind a grille at the bottom or on top. If you cannot see light through the fins, it is blocked. Grease plus flour plus dust is the usual recipe in a kitchen.
  • Clearance around the unit. Boxes stacked against a condensing unit recirculate its own hot air back through it. Give it the gap the nameplate asks for.
  • A thermometer in the product, not in the air. Air temperature swings every time the door opens. Put a probe in a container of sauce or a tub of dairy and read that — it is also the number a health inspector cares about.

The 41&deg;F line, and what to do when you cross it

The FDA Food Code sets 41°F as the upper limit for cold holding of time/temperature control for safety foods, and the Texas Food Establishment Rules follow it. The practical rule most operators are taught is the four-hour window: food held above 41°F for under four hours can generally be cooled back down or used, and food above that threshold for longer than four hours is discarded. Your own HACCP plan and your local health authority have the final word — we are refrigeration contractors, not your food safety consultant. What we can tell you is the engineering half: write down the time you first saw the reading, because the clock starts there and nobody can reconstruct it later.

Move product before you troubleshoot. A walk-in that is climbing will keep climbing while you look for the problem, and an hour spent on the ladder is an hour the inventory does not have.

When it stops being a maintenance item

Call the same day, not next week, if the box is above 41°F and still climbing; if the compressor is running continuously without satisfying; if you can smell electrical burning near the condensing unit; or if a breaker has tripped more than once. Those four are not early warnings any more — they are the failure happening.

FAQ

Questions We Get Asked

How often should commercial refrigeration be serviced in a DFW kitchen?

Quarterly for anything in a cooking environment. Grease aerosol and flour load a condenser far faster than dust alone, and a kitchen condenser that goes a year between cleanings is usually the reason a compressor fails early. Equipment in a dry stockroom can often go twice a year.

My walk-in reads 45°F but the food still feels cold. Is that an emergency?

Treat it as one. Product temperature lags air temperature, so food can still feel cold while the box has already been out of range for hours. Put a probe in the product, write down the time you first saw the reading, and move anything you cannot afford to lose.

Is short cycling actually damaging, or just annoying?

Damaging. Each start draws several times the running current and the motor windings get the heat. A compressor that short cycles for weeks often fails outright, and by then the repair is a compressor rather than whatever small fault started the cycling.
