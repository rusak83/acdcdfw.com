Short Answer

Why Ice Machines Stop Making Ice

Most ice machines stop making ice for one of five reasons: restricted water supply or a clogged filter, mineral scale on the evaporator that prevents the ice from releasing, a dust-blocked condenser, a failed harvest component, or a bin control that wrongly reports the bin as full. In Dallas–Fort Worth, water quality causes the majority of these calls.

That is the whole page in four sentences. The rest explains how to tell which one you have, what you can check yourself in ten minutes, what a repair costs, and when a machine is worth replacing instead.

Two things make ice machines different from every other appliance in the building. First, they are food equipment — ice is a food under the FDA Food Code, the machine is a food-contact surface, and a health inspector treats a slimy bin the same way they treat a dirty prep table. Second, they concentrate whatever is in the water: freezing takes pure water out of the sump and leaves every dissolved mineral behind, cycle after cycle. That is why the same machine that runs for years in soft water scales up here in a season.

Do This First

Five Checks Before You Pay for a Service Call

These take about ten minutes, need no tools, and resolve a meaningful share of the calls we get. Work them in order.

  1. Confirm water is arriving. Check that the supply valve behind or under the machine is fully open and the line is not kinked or crushed against a wall. A machine that runs a full cycle and drops nothing is very often a water problem, not a refrigeration problem.
  2. Check the age of the water filter. A filter past its interval starves the machine, and the symptom is falling production rather than an obvious no-water fault. If nobody can say when it was last changed, that is your answer. This is the single most common avoidable service call on ice equipment.
  3. Empty the bin completely. Ice bridging across the bin sensor, or a mechanical paddle stuck behind a fused block, reads to the machine as “bin full” and stops production. If the machine starts a cycle once the bin is clear, the bin control is your fault — not the refrigeration.
  4. Pull the front panel and look at the condenser. On an air-cooled machine the coil sits at kitchen or bar level and collects everything in the room. If you cannot see light through the fins, that is throttling your production. While the panel is off, check clearances: a machine boxed into a cabinet recirculates its own hot discharge air and loses output on a hot afternoon.
  5. Look inside at the evaporator and trough. Scale looks like white or grey crust on the plate; biofilm looks like grey-black slime in the trough and distribution tube. Either one means a clean is overdue. If you find slime, stop serving the ice until it is cleaned.

Still no ice after those five? It is a service call — and you will have narrowed it enough that the visit is shorter. Call (469) 224-0577 and lead with what you found.

What the panel does not tell you

None of the five faults above show up as an alarm on most machines. Production falls off gradually, ice quality slides, and the first real signal is a bar running out on a Friday night. That is why the interval matters more than the symptom — by the time there is a symptom, you have been losing output for weeks.

The DFW Problem

Scale: Why North Texas Is Hard on Ice Machines

Ice machines fail faster in hard water because freezing removes pure water and leaves dissolved minerals behind in the sump. Those minerals plate onto the evaporator, the ice stops releasing cleanly, harvest cycles stretch, cubes come out cloudy and hollow, and the machine eventually locks out. Dallas–Fort Worth tap water is moderately hard, which makes descaling a schedule item here, not an occasional repair.

The progression is predictable, and you can place your own machine on it:

  • Early: cubes lose clarity, edges look frosted rather than glassy.
  • Middle: cubes come out smaller or hollow; the freeze cycle takes noticeably longer; production per day drops.
  • Late: the slab will not release, harvest stalls, and the machine latches a safety lockout. This is the point where people call.
  • Damage: a plated evaporator left scaled long enough gets pitted, and the plate becomes a part order rather than a cleaning.

Clean it with the right chemical, or do not clean it

Ice machine evaporators are nickel-plated. Vinegar, CLR and general-purpose descalers attack that plating — a “cheap clean” can turn a maintenance job into an evaporator replacement. The correct product is a nickel-safe ice machine cleaner, followed by a separate sanitizer on the food-contact surfaces. They are two different chemicals doing two different jobs, and a clean that skips the second one leaves the biofilm behind.

How often, realistically

Manufacturers specify a full clean-and-sanitize at least twice a year. In DFW water, that is a floor rather than a target: a high-volume bar, a machine on unfiltered supply, or a unit in a hot kitchen earns three to four. Condenser cleaning runs on the same schedule or more often in a grease-laden kitchen, and the water filter changes on the manufacturer's interval — not when production drops.

This is what “serviced” looks like

A clean is not a wipe-down of the bin. Trough, distribution tube, evaporator plate, curtain, bin and drain all come apart, get cleaned with the nickel-safe product, then get sanitized separately. If a vendor is in and out in fifteen minutes without removing anything, you did not get one.

Health Code

Ice Is a Food. Inspectors Treat It That Way.

Under the FDA Food Code, which Texas Food Establishment Rules follow, ice for consumption is a food and the ice machine is food equipment. That makes the interior a food-contact surface subject to cleaning requirements, and a slimy bin is a citable finding in a routine inspection — regardless of whether the machine is producing normally.

The practical consequences for an operator in Dallas or Tarrant County:

  • Pink or black slime in the bin, on the curtain or in the trough is biofilm. It is a sanitation finding, and it is visible to an inspector who lifts the lid.
  • A machine that looks clean at the bin can still be fouled upstream. Inspectors know where to look; so do we.
  • A scoop stored in the bin, or a bin used to chill bottles, is a separate finding on the same visit.
  • Cleaning records are the difference between “we maintain it” and proving it. Every visit we run produces a written service record — equipment, work performed, date — which is the document an inspector asks for.

None of that is unique to us; it is how the rules read. What it means commercially is that ice machine service is not discretionary maintenance you defer to next quarter — it is the cheapest compliance you buy all year.

The Decision

Repair or Replace an Ice Machine

Ice machines are worth repairing longer than most people assume, because the cabinet, bin and water system routinely outlive the parts that fail. The two things that flip the answer to replace are a pitted or damaged evaporator plate on an older head, and a discontinued control board — at which point the question stops being what it costs and becomes whether the part still exists.

What actually decides it, in order:

  • Which part failed, not the machine's age. A ten-year-old head with a failed inlet valve, contactor or fan motor is a cheap repair on equipment built to keep going. The same head with a scaled-through evaporator is a different conversation.
  • Whether parts are still made. On discontinued boards and proprietary evaporators, availability decides it. We tell you that before you spend the diagnostic, not after the part is on order.
  • Refrigerant. Older equipment on legacy refrigerant carries a repair cost that modern equipment does not. Machines on current refrigerant almost always favour repair.
  • Whether the machine was ever the right size. A unit that has been running at capacity every day of its life is not failing — it is undersized, and replacing it with the same model buys the same problem again.

You get the comparison in writing before anything is ordered. If replacing is the better spend we say so, the same way we do on compressor jobs, where the honest answer is often not the bigger invoice.

Why is my ice machine not making ice?

Check the water supply, the filter age and the bin first — those three resolve a real share of calls without a technician.

Can I clean an ice machine with vinegar?

No. Ice machine evaporators are nickel-plated and vinegar, CLR and general-purpose descalers attack that plating — a cheap clean can turn into an evaporator replacement. Use a nickel-safe ice machine cleaner for scale, then a separate sanitizer on the food-contact surfaces. They are two different chemicals doing two different jobs.

Why is my ice cloudy, hollow or soft?

Almost always scale on the evaporator plate, water quality, or a spent filter. As mineral builds up the ice stops releasing cleanly, the freeze cycle stretches, and cubes come out smaller, cloudy or hollow. Change the filter first; if quality does not recover within a day or two, the plate needs descaling with a nickel-safe cleaner.

There's slime in my ice machine bin. Is the ice safe?

Stop using it and get the machine cleaned. Pink or black slime is biofilm growing on food-contact surfaces.
