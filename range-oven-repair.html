Short Answer

Why an Oven Stops Heating

On an electric or dual-fuel oven it is almost always one of four things: a failed bake element, a drifted temperature sensor, a relay on the control board that is no longer closing, or a tripped thermal cut-out. On a gas oven it is usually the hot-surface igniter — and the tell is that it still glows while the burner never lights.

That last point is the single most misdiagnosed fault in this category. A gas oven igniter does not spark the flame; it has to draw enough current to open the safety gas valve. As igniters age they keep glowing but stop drawing enough, so the sequence looks like this: the igniter glows for several minutes, gas never flows, and eventually the oven gives up. People see a glowing igniter, conclude it works, and go looking at gas supply. It is the igniter.

Worth knowing before you call: if the oven died during or just after a self-clean cycle, say so. Self-clean runs the cavity far hotter than cooking does and is a well-known way to take out a thermal fuse, an element or a control board. That one sentence points the diagnosis immediately and often shortens the visit.

Do This First

Four Checks Before You Pay for a Service Call

  1. Check the breaker properly. An electric oven runs on a double-pole breaker, and one leg can trip while the other stays live — which leaves the display and clock working perfectly while nothing heats. It looks exactly like a control board failure and it is free to rule out. Switch the breaker fully off and back on.
  2. Rule out the modes. Sabbath mode, demo mode, delayed start and control lock all produce an oven that is “broken” while behaving exactly as instructed. More common than people expect, especially after a power cut reset the panel.
  3. Put an independent thermometer on the middle rack. Preheat fully, wait fifteen minutes, then compare with the display. If the oven reads 350°F and the thermometer says 310°F, you have a sensor or calibration fault — useful to know before anyone opens the appliance, and it is at the inexpensive end of the list.
  4. On gas burners, pull and clean the caps. Boil-over blocks the ports, and a cap that is not seated flat after cleaning will not light or burns lopsided. This is the most common gas cooktop call that needs no parts at all.

Still not right? Call (469) 224-0577 and lead with the symptom, whether it's gas or electric, and any error code on the display.

Don't: force a latched self-clean door, run a gas burner that keeps sparking after it lights, or keep using an oven that trips the breaker. All three turn a repair into a bigger repair.

The Decision

Repair or Replace Cooking Equipment

On built-in wall ovens and professional ranges, repair wins in most cases — a wall oven is fitted to a specific cutout, so replacing it often drags cabinetry work along with it, and the common failures are elements, igniters, sensors and relays rather than whole-appliance failure.

Where the answer flips is parts availability. On a discontinued control board for an older unit, the question stops being what it costs and becomes whether the part still exists — and we tell you that before you spend the diagnostic, not after something is on order. On a freestanding range at the budget end, a major control failure can genuinely cost more than the appliance is worth, and we will say so.

One more thing worth weighing on professional ranges: these are appliances built to be rebuilt. Burners, valves, igniters and elements are serviceable parts on equipment that is expected to last decades. Judging one by the standards of a freestanding range sells it short.

FAQ

Range and Oven Repair Questions

My gas oven igniter glows but the burner never lights. Isn't the igniter fine?

No — that is the classic failure, and a glowing igniter is not a working igniter. A hot-surface igniter has to draw enough current to open the safety gas valve. As it ages it keeps glowing but the current draw falls below the threshold, so gas never flows. The igniter glows for several minutes, nothing lights, and the oven eventually gives up. It is one of the most commonly misdiagnosed faults in the category.

My oven display works but nothing heats. What should I check first?

The breaker, properly. An electric oven runs on a double-pole breaker and one leg can trip while the other stays live — leaving the clock and display working perfectly while the elements get nothing. It looks exactly like a control board failure. Switch the breaker fully off and back on before anything else. After that, check the oven isn't in Sabbath mode, demo mode, delayed start or control lock.

My oven stopped working right after a self-clean cycle. Is that related?

Almost certainly. Self-clean runs the cavity far hotter than normal cooking and is a well-known way to take out a thermal fuse, a bake element or a control board. Tell us when you call — it points the diagnosis straight at the components that cycle punishes and usually shortens the visit.

My oven runs cold even though the display says it's at temperature. Why?

Usually a drifted temperature sensor. The oven runs off a resistance probe in the cavity, and as it drifts the control receives a temperature that is not the real one — so the oven sits well below setpoint while insisting it has arrived. If your baking has gone wrong gradually rather than suddenly, and an independent oven thermometer disagrees with the display, that is the first suspect and it is at the inexpensive end of the list.

My gas burner clicks but won't light. Is that a part?

Often not. Blocked burner ports from a boil-over, or a burner cap that is not seated flat after cleaning, account for a large share of these calls and need no parts at all. Pull the cap, clean the ports, and make sure the cap sits flush. If it still will not light, the igniter electrode or spark module is next.

One induction zone is dead but the others work. Is the board gone?

Usually not. A single dead zone points at that zone's coil or its flex connector rather than the whole control board. Each zone gets tested independently before anything is quoted, because boards on induction cooktops are among the pricier parts on the appliance. It is also worth confirming the pan is actually ferrous and wide enough — a fair share of "zone not working" calls turn out to be cookware.

Is a professional range worth repairing?

Usually yes, and by a wider margin than people assume. Pro ranges are built to be rebuilt — burners, valves, igniters and elements are serviceable parts on equipment expected to last decades. The case for replacing is normally parts availability on a discontinued board, not the cost of the repair itself.
